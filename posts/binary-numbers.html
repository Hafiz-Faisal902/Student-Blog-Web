Binary: how computers actually count

Every number, letter, image, and sound a computer handles eventually comes down to just two symbols: 0 and 1. Not because computers are simple-minded, but because 0 and 1 map perfectly onto off and on — and off/on is the one thing a tiny electronic switch (a transistor) can reliably do, billions of times a second.

We count in decimal (base 10) because we grew up with 10 fingers to count on. Computers count in binary (base 2) because they only have two reliable states: off and on.

  1. 1

    Decimal: powers of 10

    In a decimal number, each position is worth 10× the position to its right — ones, tens, hundreds, thousands.

    Example: the number 247 breaks down as (2×100) + (4×10) + (7×1).
  2. 2

    Binary: powers of 2

    Binary works the exact same way, just with base 2 instead of base 10. Each position is worth 2× the position to its right — ones, twos, fours, eights, sixteens, and so on.

    Example: the binary number 1011 means (1×8) + (0×4) + (1×2) + (1×1) = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11 in decimal.
  3. 3

    Counting up in binary

    Just like decimal "rolls over" from 9 to 10, binary rolls over from 1 to 10 (which is decimal 2). Here's decimal 0 through 8 written in binary:

    0 = 0000  ·  1 = 0001  ·  2 = 0010  ·  3 = 0011  ·  4 = 0100  ·  5 = 0101  ·  6 = 0110  ·  7 = 0111  ·  8 = 1000

Each individual 0 or 1 is called a bit (short for "binary digit"). Group 8 of them together and you get a byte — the unit almost every file size is measured in, from kilobytes to gigabytes.
