How I'm getting ready for exams this term

Exams are two weeks out. Last term, my "studying" was mostly re-reading the same notes over and over until they felt familiar. It felt productive. It wasn't — familiar isn't the same as knowing something well enough to answer a question about it under pressure. This term I changed the actual method, not just the number of hours.

Mapping out what I actually know first

Before opening a single book, I go through each subject and mark every chapter as red (can't explain or solve it), yellow (understand it partly, still make mistakes), or green (can answer without help). Then I give my first study sessions to the red chapters, not the green ones.

This sounds obvious written down, but it's the opposite of what I used to do — I'd keep revising the subjects that already felt comfortable, because it felt productive without being effortful. The chapters I couldn't touch were exactly the ones I kept avoiding.

Studying by testing myself, not re-reading

The single biggest change: after reading a topic once, I close the book and try to write or say everything I remember about it before checking if I got it right. This is called retrieval practice, and it's one of the few study techniques that's actually backed by research comparing different methods head to head — it consistently beats re-reading, even though it feels harder and slower in the moment.

💡 Trick: if you can't explain a topic out loud, in your own words, without glancing at your notes — you don't actually know it yet, no matter how many times you've read it. Try explaining it to a sibling or a wall. Both work fine.

Keeping a mistake notebook

Instead of just correcting a wrong answer and moving on, I write it down: the question, what I answered, why it was wrong, and the correct method. "Careless mistake" doesn't count as a reason — I have to be specific, like "forgot the negative sign" or "used the wrong formula for this case."

I go back through that notebook two days later, and once more right before the exam. It turns every mistake I make while practicing into part of my actual revision material, instead of something I just feel briefly annoyed about and forget.

Rotating subjects instead of marathon sessions

I used to study one subject for an entire evening. Now each subject gets a couple of focused sessions spread across different days, rather than one long block. Coming back to a topic after a day or two — instead of cramming it once — is called spaced practice, and it's the reason this actually sticks instead of leaking out of my head by the following week.

Fixing the real distraction

My actual problem was never "not enough study time" — it was open- ended phone scrolling with no natural stopping point. I've replaced that with a fixed cutoff and a short, specific list of what I'm actually allowed to do on my phone in the evening, rather than just picking it up and seeing where the algorithm takes me. Having no decision to make in the moment (the list is already decided) makes it much easier to actually stop.

Project work doesn't stop — it just shrinks

I'm also building a personal AI project on the side, and my first instinct during exam season was to feel guilty about barely touching it. I've stopped doing that. For these two weeks it gets a small amount of time on a few days instead of daily attention, and goes back to normal once exams are done. Exams have a fixed deadline; the project doesn't. Temporarily de-prioritizing something isn't the same as giving up on it.

The actual goal

It's tempting to treat exam prep like a competition to sound like you're doing the most — more hours, more highlighters, more color-coded notes. None of that is really the goal. The goal is being someone who can stop on time, notice a mistake instead of brushing past it, and show up again the next day and do it again. That's the part that actually carries over past the exam — into projects, work, and just being someone people can rely on.

💡 One habit I've kept: every Sunday, I rate myself 1–5 on focus, decision quality, self-control, how much schoolwork actually got done, and progress on my project. Takes two minutes, and it's the only reason I can tell if a week actually went well or just felt busy.

If you've got a study method that's actually worked for you, drop it in the comments below — always looking for one more good one.
